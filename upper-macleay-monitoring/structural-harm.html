UPPER MACLEAY FLOOD MONITORING · STRUCTURAL HARM ANALYSIS

Why fragmented flood and access information can be dangerous

Terminology control: SHM means Structural Harm Mechanism in the MAYHEM framework. The MAYHEM Structural Harm Mechanism Catalogue v3.0 records 96 investigation-discovered mechanisms in one unified catalogue. The SH-01–SH-28 identifiers shown here preserve the Upper Macleay source references; they correspond to catalogue mechanisms 069–096.

This analysis identifies mechanisms by which fragmented, indirect, inaccessible or poorly contextualised information can degrade community situational awareness before, during and after flooding. It does not determine official road safety status or claim that a mechanism has caused a particular injury.

Structural harm pathway

Fragmented monitoring and access information → incomplete situational picture → interpretation burden transferred to residents → increased ambiguity and information latency → potential delayed or incorrect access decisions → prolonged or poorly understood isolation → increased exposure to medical, supply and access consequences.

Evidence discipline: This is mechanism analysis. It distinguishes observed institutional processes from proposed control architecture. Official warnings, bridge inspection, closure, reopening and engineering decisions remain with the responsible authorities.

Identified structural harm mechanisms

SH-17

Medical-access isolation

Flood isolation can interrupt ordinary access to medication, medical appointments, wound care, diagnostic services and other health support. NSW State Emergency Service (NSW SES) guidance expressly treats medications, medical supplies and transport to medical appointments as isolation/resupply needs.

Potential effect: an access delay can become a health-service access problem rather than merely a transport inconvenience.

SH-18

Hydrological clearance is not access restoration

A falling river or water clearing the bridge deck does not establish that the crossing is open. Kempsey Shire Council states that after flooding a road is not reopened until the bridge has been inspected and debris cleared.

Potential effect: residents can observe apparently improved hydrological conditions while the legal and operational access state remains CLOSED.

SH-19

Hidden post-flood decision queue

The resident may see water recede without visibility of the operational sequence behind reopening: inspection scheduling, structural inspection, debris clearance, defect identification, repair escalation, reopening authority and public status publication.

Potential effect: uncertainty persists after the visible flood hazard has reduced because the next decision point is institutional rather than hydrological.

SH-20

Post-warning information gap

A formal flood warning can end before local access is restored. Kempsey Shire Council's 5 August 2025 update recorded a final flood warning and falling river while Bellbrook, Toorooka, Temagog and Sherwood bridges remained closed pending inspection and cleaning.

Potential effect: the end of the flood-warning phase can be mistaken for the end of the access-isolation phase.

SH-21

Recovery latency

The period between water clearing a crossing and official reopening is a distinct operational interval. Inspection, debris removal, damage assessment and repair can extend isolation after the hydrological trigger has passed.

Potential effect: conventional flood records can understate the duration of practical isolation if they measure only river behaviour and not access restoration.

SH-22

Inspection and debris-clearance sequencing

Multiple affected structures compete for inspection, cleaning and repair resources after an event. Council records show crossings reopening at different times and explicitly ask residents to allow staff time to clean and inspect structures.

Potential effect: restoration order becomes consequential for communities with limited or single-route access.

SH-23

Damage-dependent reopening

Inspection can identify damage that prevents reopening even after water and debris are no longer the primary constraint. In May 2025 Council recorded Dowlings Falls Causeway as remaining closed because the structure had been significantly damaged by flooding.

Potential effect: a crossing can transition from flood closure into infrastructure outage, requiring a different response and potentially a much longer restoration period.

SH-24

Secondary route failure

Restoring a bridge does not necessarily restore the whole journey. Connecting roads can remain unavailable because of landslips, fallen trees, powerlines, scour, inundation or other damage.

Potential effect: bridge status alone can create a false impression that access to services has been restored.

SH-25

Uninspected-road uncertainty

Council has warned that roads not officially closed may still be impassable where staff have not yet inspected them because of high demand.

Potential effect: absence of a recorded closure is not necessarily evidence of usable access during a widespread event.

SH-26

Isolation consequence inequality

The same closure duration can have different consequences depending on household circumstances. Residents requiring regular treatment, prescription medication, disability support, wound care or time-sensitive appointments may have a lower tolerance for extended isolation.

Potential effect: restoration latency can create disproportionate consequences even where the physical closure is identical for all residents.

SH-27

Medical resupply as a compensating control, not restored access

NSW SES can support isolated residents with essential food and medication resupply and transport to urgent medical appointments. That emergency support mitigates isolation consequences but does not mean normal road access or ordinary health-service access has been restored.

Potential effect: emergency resupply can conceal the practical severity of prolonged access disruption if it is treated as equivalent to normal connectivity.

SH-28

Missing restoration-time metric

Flood monitoring commonly records water level and warnings, while the time from hydrological clearance to official reopening is not presented as a comparable public metric.

Potential effect: recurring post-flood access delays are difficult to quantify, compare and improve.

Access-state model

The investigation should not reduce a crossing to OPEN/CLOSED. The following state model separates the hydrological event from the operational restoration process:

NORMAL → FLOOD THREAT → WATER OVER CROSSING / CLOSED → WATER RECEDING → WATER CLEAR BUT CLOSED → AWAITING INSPECTION → INSPECTED / AWAITING DEBRIS CLEARANCE → DAMAGE / REPAIR REQUIRED if applicable → ACCESS RESTORED → OPEN STATUS PUBLISHED

Measurement requirement: record the time water clears the relevant crossing, inspection time where available, debris-clearance/repair milestones where available, official reopening time, and the resulting hydrological-clearance-to-reopening interval. A claimed “three day” delay should be established from dated event evidence for the particular crossing, not generalised without records.

Decision points that should be visible

  • What observation or threshold triggers closure?
  • What observation permits inspection to commence?
  • Who requests, schedules and performs the inspection?
  • What inspection standard determines whether cleaning alone is sufficient?
  • Who removes debris and how is that work prioritised across multiple crossings?
  • What defects trigger engineering assessment or repair rather than immediate reopening?
  • Who has authority to declare the crossing open?
  • When and where is the OPEN status published?
  • How are connecting-road failures checked before access is treated as restored?
  • How are single-access communities and medically consequential isolation considered in restoration priority?

SOP / procedure accountability request

The evidence site does not assume an undocumented Council procedure. The submission should request the current documented procedure, SOP, work instruction or equivalent operational process governing post-flood bridge reopening.

Requested fields: closure trigger; inspection trigger; inspection responsibility; prioritisation criteria; debris-clearance responsibility; structural-damage escalation; engineering sign-off where required; reopening authority; status-publication process; treatment of connecting-road hazards; and whether single-access, emergency-service or medically consequential isolation affects priority.

Evidence rule: if Council supplies a documented process, compare actual event records against it. If no documented process exists, record that response as a finding rather than inferring a procedure.

Why Bellbrook Floodwatch is relevant

Bellbrook Floodwatch demonstrates a persistent crossing-level information architecture. The next architectural step is to distinguish water state from access state rather than implying that a falling gauge equals restored access.

A community-facing system can preserve river observations, camera history and source provenance while separately displaying authoritative closure/reopening status and post-flood restoration milestones where those data are made available.

Design principle: the objective is not to predict when Council will reopen a bridge. It is to make the known state and decision boundary visible: hydrological condition, official closure status, inspection/cleaning/repair status where published, and authoritative reopening.

Implementation implications

  • Define which monitoring source serves each critical crossing.
  • Expose suitable existing data and validate proxy relationships.
  • Integrate authoritative road/bridge status without conflating it with gauge readings.
  • Record post-flood restoration milestones and hydrological-clearance-to-reopening time.
  • Publish inspection, cleaning and repair status where operationally appropriate.
  • Assess isolation consequences, including medical-service access, when prioritising critical corridors.
  • Check connecting roads before treating bridge reopening as full access restoration.
  • Design monitoring, communications and restoration-status publication into new critical infrastructure.
